Vitrine skin

Frosted panes over a dark scene: translucent tiles with a real backdrop-filter blur, a vivid spring-green accent, and generous radii. The toggle below stamps data-skin="vitrine" on the document — no markup on this page changes, only the stamp. The theme toggle in the nav still works, because a skin is orthogonal to the theme: it carries its own light and dark halves, so there are four combinations to check rather than two.

Dark is the faithful half and light is a design decision, the arrangement midgard records. The reference is a dark scene lit from within; inverting it gives a pale ground under a green that cannot be read on it, which is what the stepped light accent roles exist to avoid.

The tint is a third attribute, and it lights the scene too

data-skin="vitrine" × data-tint="<hue>" × data-theme. Five hues — azure, cyan, violet, magenta, amber — and the default is the absence of data-tint, not a value of it, so the shipped spring green keeps its hand-derived values and nothing that does not stamp the attribute can change. It is data-tint rather than industry's data-hazard because this accent is a material, not a signal — the colour the glass is lit by — and the attribute name is the first place a consumer reads what a value means.

What moves is the accent namespace and the default scene's brand pools. Industry's hazard swap touches colour tokens only; here the backdrop behind the glass carries the accent as two radial pools, so a violet accent over a green scene would read as a mistake. The re-hue is free by construction: vitrine-clamp compresses whatever sits behind the panes into a fixed luminance band, so no ratio in the token file depends on the scene's hue. The teal companion pool and the diagonal rule do not move — they are the scene's second light, not the accent.

Every value is derived by search, not picked, by industry's own method: one HSL anchor plus a 1% lightness ramp, each role the nearest legal step to the hue's vivid centre. Run against the shipped green it returns the accent fill exactly and lands within a step or two on the light roles. Two of industry's findings reproduce here on a completely different palette: “the fill cannot bound itself” is a pale-hue rule, per half — in light azure, violet and magenta are their own border-accent while cyan and amber need a stepped one, and in dark that inverts for violet — and “black ink always” is not a rule at all: cyan and amber take near-black, violet takes white in both halves, and azure and magenta disagree between their own two halves.

Amber carries a restriction An amber accent and this skin's amber warning separate only by lightness, so under deuteranopia the accent collapses into the status set (ΔE 8.9 against status.warning.mark, 7.0 against status.serious.mark, floor 10 — acknowledged in the token file and gated). With data-tint="amber": a status badge carries icon and label, a delta states direction in text, and an amber CTA does not share a tile with a warning chip whose only mark is its colour. All four cool tints are clear of the whole status set; this is the price of the warm one.

The glass, and why it can be measured at all

Chrome is glass; content is solid. Frosting is a property of things that sit above what you are reading — the masthead, the submodule nav, menus, modals. Content is what you are reading, and it needs a ground that does not move. The tiles below take opaque surface-default; the scene shows around them, which is where it was always doing its work.

A translucent card takes its colour from whatever is behind it, so a tall card over a photograph read as a vertical gradient — pale where the sky was, flushed where the ground was — and the small tiles inside it then sat on three different grounds. Every ratio still passed, because they are measured against the composite’s extremes and the extremes were honoured. It still looked wrong: a pane whose tint changes down its own height reads as a rendering artefact rather than as a material, and no contrast figure sees that.

The clamp failed at both ends, which is why opacity was the fix rather than a retune. Routed through --jrk-vitrine-backdrop, contrast(0.18) brightness(1.85) lands every channel in [193, 255] — a 62-level window at the top of the range. A token-built pastel gradient survives it; a photograph is flattened until there is nothing left to blur, and the glass renders as white cardboard. Painted on a wrapper instead, the clamp never runs at all and the panes take the image at full strength. An opaque plane is surface-default whatever is behind it, so these tiles’ measurements are exactly true rather than true-within-a-band.

What that does not fix is ink that sits on the page. A title, a description, an unselected nav label — none of those are on a card. On the clamped page text-muted measures 4.82–5.70:1, which is what validate:skin gates. On an unclamped sunset it is 3.85:1 on the yellow, 2.10:1 on the orange, 1.37:1 on the pink and 1.12:1 on the violet, and text-primary drops to 2.93:1. Unreadable, not merely poor. The backdrop must go through the token — a scene painted on a wrapper voids every page-level guarantee here and nothing detects it.

surface-default is still a recording for what remains glass — the masthead, the overlays, the selected tab below — so “measure against both extremes, never the recorded middle” is unchanged for those. It simply no longer reaches content. And this is the cheap direction: backdrop-filter is a full-viewport composite per element, so a dashboard of twenty tiles was paying twenty of them for an effect that was hurting it.

The submodule nav is a raised pane, not a rule

Base draws .jrk-tabs as paper — a hairline across the row and a 3px rule under the selected label. That is right on a page made of sheets and wrong here, where nothing else in the skin states anything with a rule; on glass the row read as un-skinned. The selected tab now takes surface-glass, the blur and shadow-card’s top-edge catch, and both the row’s hairline and the tab’s underline go to transparent — colour only, never border: 0, which kills border-style so a later border-color paints nothing.

This is why surface-glass still has a consumer now that content is opaque — and it takes the plain value rather than surface-glass-raised deliberately: raised belongs to things that float over the page, and a tab is on it.

The state keeps a 3:1 channel, which is what makes dropping the underline safe. An inset 1px border-accent rim replaces it — 3.28:1 on this skin’s panel — drawn as an inset box-shadow rather than a border, because base reserves 3px of transparent bottom border to stop the row shifting on selection and a real border would redo that reservation and move every label. Then the pane, then semibold, again the only channel that survives greyscale and both dichromacies. accent-text at 4.66:1 reinforces; it does not substitute.

:not(.jrk-tabs--pills) is load-bearing. Pills are the same widget as .jrk-btn-group in a second markup contract and must not be repainted here — and the guard is also what keeps the two forms distinguishable under this skin: pills are a well, tabs are a flat row with one pane lifted out of it. .jrk-section-nav was checked and deliberately left alone. Its current-state mark looks like the same paper idiom and is not — the ::after is a scaled accent-solid dot, not a rule, and ink and semibold already sit beside it. Killing it would have removed a working signal to fix a problem it did not have.

The rounded contract

industry and midgard each name a list of controls that must be squared. This skin inverts that — but the radius tokens alone round nothing, because the base library reaches for radius.none directly on tiles rather than through radius-lg. So the tiles have to be named, and that list is this skin's contract: anything new stays square until it is added to it.

Controls need no rule at all here. They take radius-full or radius-md from the base layer and both already suit a soft look — which is why this stylesheet is shorter than the other two skins have to be. A rounded skin agrees with the base control ladder where a squared one has to fight it.

Passing Review

Nested panes take no blur

Two frosted layers over each other read as fog rather than as depth, the inner one has nothing behind it worth blurring, and the composite cost is paid twice for it. A nested tile takes the opaque composite and the neutral hairline nesting.css already gives it.

The backdrop belongs to the app, never to the library

This library has never shipped a binary, and the portal's sync script mirrors whole directories — so a photograph dropped into css/ would be copied into the app on every sync forever, referenced or not. The skin exposes a hook instead and ships a token-built ramp as its default, so it looks like itself standalone: 

:root[data-skin='vitrine'] {
  --jrk-vitrine-backdrop: url('/factory.webp');
}

Set it in this page's devtools to watch the scrim do its job: the panes stay legible over anything you point it at, which is the entire purpose of bounding the composite.

The glass over a photograph

The section above sets the backdrop for the whole viewport. This one carries its own, because that is what a real app looks like — a hero with panes floating on it — and because six percent of an image is nothing to blur. A gradient has no high-frequency detail, so it is the weakest possible test of frosted glass: the blur has nothing to smear. Point it at a photograph and the material finally reads.

Why this pane stays legible

The photograph is clamped by --jrk-vitrine-clamp before it reaches the glass — by luminance, not by coverage — so it keeps its structure while the composite stays inside the band every ink here was solved against. The pane itself is surface.glass plus the blur, never surface.default: that token is the composite this fill produces.
