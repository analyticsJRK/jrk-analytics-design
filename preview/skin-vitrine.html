Vitrine skin

Frosted panes over a dark scene: translucent tiles with a real backdrop-filter blur, a vivid spring-green accent, and generous radii. The toggle below stamps data-skin="vitrine" on the document — no markup on this page changes, only the stamp. The theme toggle in the nav still works, because a skin is orthogonal to the theme: it carries its own light and dark halves, so there are four combinations to check rather than two.

Dark is the faithful half and light is a design decision, the arrangement midgard records. The reference is a dark scene lit from within; inverting it gives a pale ground under a green that cannot be read on it, which is what the stepped light accent roles exist to avoid.

The tint is a third attribute, and it lights the scene too

data-skin="vitrine" × data-tint="<hue>" × data-theme. Five hues — azure, cyan, violet, magenta, amber — and the default is the absence of data-tint, not a value of it, so the shipped spring green keeps its hand-derived values and nothing that does not stamp the attribute can change. It is data-tint rather than industry's data-hazard because this accent is a material, not a signal — the colour the glass is lit by — and the attribute name is the first place a consumer reads what a value means.

What moves is the accent namespace and the default scene's brand pools. Industry's hazard swap touches colour tokens only; here the backdrop behind the glass carries the accent as two radial pools, so a violet accent over a green scene would read as a mistake. The re-hue is free by construction: vitrine-clamp compresses whatever sits behind the panes into a fixed luminance band, so no ratio in the token file depends on the scene's hue. The teal companion pool and the diagonal rule do not move — they are the scene's second light, not the accent.

Every value is derived by search, not picked, by industry's own method: one HSL anchor plus a 1% lightness ramp, each role the nearest legal step to the hue's vivid centre. Run against the shipped green it returns the accent fill exactly and lands within a step or two on the light roles. Two of industry's findings reproduce here on a completely different palette: “the fill cannot bound itself” is a pale-hue rule, per half — in light azure, violet and magenta are their own border-accent while cyan and amber need a stepped one, and in dark that inverts for violet — and “black ink always” is not a rule at all: cyan and amber take near-black, violet takes white in both halves, and azure and magenta disagree between their own two halves.

Amber carries a restriction An amber accent and this skin's amber warning separate only by lightness, so under deuteranopia the accent collapses into the status set (ΔE 8.9 against status.warning.mark, 7.0 against status.serious.mark, floor 10 — acknowledged in the token file and gated). With data-tint="amber": a status badge carries icon and label, a delta states direction in text, and an amber CTA does not share a tile with a warning chip whose only mark is its colour. All four cool tints are clear of the whole status set; this is the price of the warm one.

The glass, and why it can be measured at all

A translucent tile has no fixed ink ground, which would void every contrast guarantee in the library. validate-skin.mjs refuses it outright — “surface-default is not a flat colour; every other check measures against it”. So surface-default records what the glass composites to, as a flat hex, and the gate goes on measuring all 532 checks against it exactly as it would for an opaque skin. The translucency lives in surface-glass and is used only for rendering.

That recording is only honest because the scrim bounds it. A consumer points --jrk-vitrine-backdrop at their own image; the scrim is the veil between it and the glass. At alpha 0.94 the composite spans #151c1b to #19201f in dark whatever sits behind it — black through white — and every ink was solved against the pale extreme rather than against the recorded hex. At 0.90 the span widened enough to put text-muted at 4.46:1, under by 0.04 and invisible to the gate, which only ever sees the flat value. Backdrop, scrim, glass alpha and surface-default are one decision in four places.

The rounded contract

industry and midgard each name a list of controls that must be squared. This skin inverts that — but the radius tokens alone round nothing, because the base library reaches for radius.none directly on tiles rather than through radius-lg. So the tiles have to be named, and that list is this skin's contract: anything new stays square until it is added to it.

Controls need no rule at all here. They take radius-full or radius-md from the base layer and both already suit a soft look — which is why this stylesheet is shorter than the other two skins have to be. A rounded skin agrees with the base control ladder where a squared one has to fight it.

Passing Review

Nested panes take no blur

Two frosted layers over each other read as fog rather than as depth, the inner one has nothing behind it worth blurring, and the composite cost is paid twice for it. A nested tile takes the opaque composite and the neutral hairline nesting.css already gives it.

The backdrop belongs to the app, never to the library

This library has never shipped a binary, and the portal's sync script mirrors whole directories — so a photograph dropped into css/ would be copied into the app on every sync forever, referenced or not. The skin exposes a hook instead and ships a token-built ramp as its default, so it looks like itself standalone: 

:root[data-skin='vitrine'] {
  --jrk-vitrine-backdrop: url('/factory.webp');
}

Set it in this page's devtools to watch the scrim do its job: the panes stay legible over anything you point it at, which is the entire purpose of bounding the composite.
