Components

Every element on this page is plain HTML plus a jrk-* class — no framework, no build step. The React wrappers emit exactly these class names.

The picker stamps data-skin on the document and nothing else — no markup on this page changes. It is orthogonal to the theme toggle in the nav, because a skin is not a third theme: each carries its own light and dark halves, so every skin here is four combinations to check, not two.

Skin
Each skin has a dedicated page with its own notes and, for industry, its hazard-hue picker: Industry · Midgard · Vitrine.
Accent hue
Stamps data-accent on the document. Blue is the absence of the attribute, not a value of it, so nothing that does not stamp it changes by one value. The axis is scoped :root:not([data-skin]) — a skin owns its own accent, which is what data-hazard and data-tint are, so picking a skin above disables this row.
Only the accent namespace moves. The four positional gradient.* tile tones, the --brand masthead ramp and all eight chart slots stay where they are — the ramp carries a measured layout condition and the chart order is the colourblind-safety mechanism, and neither is re-derived per hue. So a stamped hue changes buttons, links, focus rings, selected controls and the sheet banner, and leaves every chart mark alone.

Surfaces — the tile boundary

A tile has no border. It is bounded by its fill step off the page, and both themes now use the same Apple grouped mechanism: #ffffff on #f2f2f7 in light (1.12:1), #232326 on #141416 in dark (1.17:1). That symmetry is the point — for one period light was a flat #ffffff page bounded by a 2px brand edge, so a change that read correctly in dark could be invisible in light.

Tiles still declare border: 1px solid transparent, never border: 0: the reserved width means an edge appearing changes only a colour and never reflows, and border: 0 sets border-style: none, after which border-color paints nothing at all.

default

Card fill, no border. The step to the page behind it is the whole boundary.

--interactive

Hover lifts it: a shadow (which carries light) and a fill lift (which carries dark, where a black shadow is invisible). Neither channel works in both themes.

--seamless

A no-op at the top level now — and the only way to suppress the hairline a nested tile is given.

default

Opaque. The ramp stops dead at the edge.

--frosted

72% fill over saturate(180%) blur(20px) — the topbar's material, on a tile. Muted ink is re-inked to text.secondary by the modifier: muted lands at 3.4:1 once a saturated ground shows through.

--frosted

The hairline and the shadow are the whole boundary here — this is the only tile whose fill can be weaker than the base card's, which is why --seamless and --flush must never be combined with it.

Frost is only worth spending where something is behind it. --frosted with --seamless or --flush is a contradiction: it removes the two channels holding the tile together and leaves a blur with no object around it. A link is the one ink it cannot fix — accent.text falls to 3.45:1 over a saturated ground and is already the shallowest passing step on the hue. The full hand-measured table is in card.css; nothing gates a colour composited at runtime. Both cases that earn it — a saturated ground and content scrolling underneath — are live on the dashboard page.

The tone treats the summary; the panel is always the card plane. Every token a table reaches for — the row hairlines, the muted caption, the accent.text header labels, every chart mark in a cell bar — was measured against surface.default. Fill the panel with a gradient and all of them are wrong at once, and nothing in the library would report it. A vivid expander is a gradient header on a white card, not a gradient card.
A card opens in place — in the column it already occupies, with its neighbours neither moving nor resizing. .jrk-expander-row used to promote an open card to the full row width and collapse to one column; that was removed on direction (2026-08-17) because the collapse re-laid the whole row and stretched every other card to page width too. The cost moved to the caller: three tiles across puts a panel in a ~410px column, where a wide table is correctly styled at a width no table can use — “947 of 982 units” wraps and rows grow three or four lines tall. Put a compact list in the panel, widen the tracks, or give a table its own full-width expander.
Three tones, three hover mechanisms, and none is interchangeable. The plain tone steps to surface.hover — a plane to the next plane. A tint has no next plane, so pastel takes overlay.hoverVeil, which darkens a light tint and lightens a dark one (1.10–1.17:1 light, 1.19–1.26:1 dark); a single white veil would lift a dark tint 1.34:1 and a light one 1.03:1 — the same gesture strong at night and absent by day. Vivid takes gradient.hover, theme-independent because the ramp is. Hue is decoration on both toned variants: assign a tone by position in the row, never by what the card is about.

Depth is vertical, peers are horizontal, and the connectors are drawn at text.muted — 5.07:1 light, 5.46:1 dark, not the 1.71:1 of border.strong. The lines are the content: once a row is wide enough that a child is no longer visibly beneath its own parent, nothing else on screen says who reports to whom. The card hairlines stay decorative at 1.52:1, which is the difference the choice turns on.
The branch under Dana is --rollup: each child takes the next palette slot and its whole subtree inherits it, so Harbor Point and Cedar Grove wear Southeast's blue and the four Midwest properties wear Priya's orange. This cycles the categorical palette, which everywhere else in this library is banned — and the ban is about identity: two lines on a chart sharing a colour cannot be told apart because colour is the only thing naming them. Here every node is labelled and the tree draws the grouping, so the keyline is an accelerator over a chart that already reads without it.
The keyline carries two channels, hue and texture — solid, dashed, dotted, double-rail, assigned ceil(slot/2). That pairing is derived, not chosen: every collapsing pair in the palette is same-parity, so pairing the slots up puts each bucket's two members an odd distance apart (always safe) and lands all nine collapsing pairs in different buckets. On hue alone the first eight groups contain 9 pairs that are the same colour to a dichromat — orange and yellow are dE 0.8. With texture that count is 0.
Adjacent siblings never collide at any count: consecutive slots are the exact pair the palette order was searched to maximise, dE 22.3 light / 16.5 dark. The ceiling is 3 × textures = 12, since only three hues here are pairwise CVD-safe — so this ships 8 and group 9 repeats group 1 exactly. That repeat is deliberate: shifting texture on a second lap removes it but buys 4 CVD collisions instead, and an exact repeat is visible to everyone while a CVD collision looks correct to the author and lands only on readers who cannot see it. The connectors stay text.muted and are never tinted: four of the eight hues sit at 2.12–2.57:1 on the light card, which is fine for a keyline beside a label and not fine for the one mark that has to be readable.
Midwest is --stacked, the escape valve for a wide fan — twelve peers side by side is 2,300px no screen holds, and the same twelve down an indented spine fit in a column. It is for leaf children only. Open it with the toggle: the count is the label, because a bare caret leaves a collapsed node as a dead end instead of an answer. The two toggles sit differently on purpose — Southeast centres on its stem, Midwest hangs off the spine, because a stacked branch moves its parent card off the node's midpoint and the connectors follow it. Mountain West is vacant, and the dashed edge is redundant coding — the words Open position are the signal. Dana carries aria-current, whose tinted wash is 1.16:1 on the light card, so the semibold name is the channel that survives greyscale and both dichromacies.

It is the weaker of the two at telling groups apart, and that is the whole trade. Tints are pastel by construction — outside the lightness band, under the chroma floor — so the validator skips them. Measured at the same dE 10 floor, both themes: the keyline's marks hold 22.3 / 16.5 on the worst adjacent pair, the tints 4.5 / 8.6. It does not fail immediately — the first four slots are clear (14.5 / 17.1) and the break is at the 4|5 pair — so four groups or fewer is safe, five warns in development. Non-adjacent groups are only guaranteed to two (1|3 is dE 6.0 / 3.9). Re-ordering does not rescue it: the best of all 40,320 permutations is dE 8.8 in light, still under the floor. This demo runs four regions for that reason.
Three details worth looking for. The root card is unfilled — it sits above the rollup, so it has no group, and every declaration in the variant falls back to the value the card already had (the plain card plane in either theme); setting the class on a chart that never groups anything changes nothing. Midwest is aria-current and keeps its group fill, stating currency on a border.accent edge instead — 3.81:1 light / 5.26:1 dark, which makes it the rare "this is the one" in this library that clears the 3:1 signifier floor, where the plain card's is a 1.76:1 hairline over a 1.16:1 wash. And the vacant card keeps its fill too: the dash says the seat is open, the fill says which rollup it heads, and both move — the dash to the group's mark, since border.strong is 1.24:1 on a tint, and the name to secondary. Three layouts for a level, and this chart uses all three of them. The top of the tree fans out, because four SVPs are compared with each other. The bottom is --column: the properties under one manager are looked up one at a time or counted, never compared, so fanning them out buys nothing and costs 2,300px. A column draws no connectors at all — it sits directly under its parent at exactly the parent's width, and containment states the relationship. That is a stronger claim than a line, because a line can be traced to the wrong card in a thirty-card row and a box under a card cannot. --stacked is the middle case: it indents children away from the parent and then spends a spine reconnecting them.
The solid fill is chart.deep, not chart.categorical, and that is the only new palette here. On the series marks no ink but black clears 4.5:1 and white clears it on none — so rather than change the ink, the fill moved: the same eight hues in the same searched order, stepped down in lightness until white reaches 4.5:1 on every slot in both halves (worst 4.54:1 light, 4.58:1 dark). It is a volume of the series palette, not a second palette, which is why none of the colourblind doctrine had to be re-argued: worst adjacent dE 15.9 light / 18.4 dark, all-pairs safe cap 3 exactly as the marks declare, and all nine collapsing pairs still same-parity — so the same (1,2)(3,4)(5,6)(7,8) texture buckets separate every one and the first eight groups stay fully distinguishable. npm run validate gates all of it.
A deep fill also bounds itself — 4.54:1 on the light card, 3.29:1 on the dark one — so a filled node is a shape without an edge. It keeps a 1px white hairline anyway, and that is not decoration: two tiles of the same hue stacked flush in a column have a 1.00:1 fill step between them, and the hairline is the only thing there. What the fill spends is the card's three levels of ink, which collapse to one — so name, code and figure separate by size and weight alone. Flatten those and this variant has nothing left.
The slots are assigned by name, not by position, and this chart is the case that requires it. --rollup derives a slot from nth-child, so it can only ever colour the children of one node; here the eight managers are spread across three different associates' child lists with no ordinal in common. .jrk-org__node--group-N lets the caller assign from the group's identity instead, so one manager keeps one colour wherever they appear. What the caller takes on is the numbering: adjacency is guaranteed between consecutive slots, so groups must be numbered in the order they are read — left to right across the level, by first appearance — never by a hash or an alphabetical key.
The apex card is the one node outside the palette, set per node with --jrk-org-group-solid and --jrk-org-solid-ink together — one alone gives white-on-white. It takes surface.inverse, and it takes it because that plane flips rather than in spite of it. What this card has to do is separate from surface.default, the plane every other card sits on, in both halves and under all four palettes — and an inverse plane is defined as the maximum step off the plane, so whichever way the theme goes it goes the other. Worst case across base / industry / midgard / vitrine × light / dark: ink 13.96:1 on the fill, 12.91:1 of step against the panel.
surface.bannerDeep looks like the better answer and is measurably the wrong one — worth recording, because it fails the test a careless check would pass it on. It reads as a stable dark navy in the base layer, which is where it gets checked first; but a skin owns its own planes, and vitrine sets it to #e9eceb in light, which is 1.025:1 against that skin's panel. The apex card vanishes into the chart. It is a banner plane, built to sit under the topbar's own ink, and nothing about it promises a step against a card. So the rule is not "prefer a themed pair" — bannerDeep is a themed pair with measured ink and it still fails. It is: this card needs a plane whose step against surface.default is guaranteed, and inverse is the only namespace that makes that promise.
The state boxes are the one thing colour could not carry. Every tile in a column is the same hue — that hue names the rollup and is already spent — so the subdivision inside it gets a bracket and a label instead. The label is neutral ink, not the group hue, and that is measured: a deep fill is 4.54:1 on the light card and 3.29:1 on the dark one, so it can be the box's edge (3:1, graphical) and cannot be its text (4.5:1) in dark — in all eight slots. The edge takes the hue, the ink takes the ink. In markup it is an <li> holding a <ul>, so a screen-reader user gets the nesting and the state name in place of the box.
Cards are centre-aligned here, reversing an earlier decision, and the reason is worth stating. The old rule was start-aligned, on the grounds that centring is the diagram-tool look and costs scanning on a wrapped name. Both observations still hold. What changed is what a reader does at this density: a level is thirty cards wide and a card is three short lines, so the comparison runs across a row and centred text gives every card in it the same optical centre. The scanning cost is real and is paid on the name, which is why the compact tile clamps its name to two lines — one long property name allowed a third would set the height for the eleven tiles beside it. The group label is the one string that stays start-aligned: it heads the list rather than belonging to the row.
Under a skin only the tile and the typeface change. Both radii come from tokens — radius.lg on the card, radius.md on the group — so industry and midgard square them and vitrine rounds them with no rule written. On top of that: midgard adds its engraved bevel and sets the full-size name in font.display (mixed case, because a tracked-caps name does not fit 176px), vitrine adds the glass catch and no blur, and industry adds nothing but the squaring — it deliberately takes no notch, because an org card is frequently a link and clip-path clips the focus ring along with the shadow. The eight fills, the ink and the connectors are inherited whole by all three: the slot order is the colourblind mechanism, so it does not re-hue for a skin, an accent or a hazard hue.
